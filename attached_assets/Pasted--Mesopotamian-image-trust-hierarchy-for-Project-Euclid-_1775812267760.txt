# Mesopotamian image trust hierarchy for Project Euclid

**The Metropolitan Museum of Art (CC0, full API) and CDLI (open REST API, 500K+ cuneiform records) are your two foundational pillars for verified, automatable image sourcing.** Together they cover the broadest range of Mesopotamian artifacts with the strongest combination of open licensing, scholar-verified metadata, and programmatic access. Every other source in this hierarchy either supplements their coverage, fills geographic gaps, or requires more manual verification. Below is the complete tiered system for Reading With Integrity compliance.

-----

## The trust framework: what makes a source RWI-safe

Six criteria determine where a source sits in this hierarchy. **Curator verification** means every caption was written or approved by a specialist — this is non-negotiable for RWI. Provenance documentation traces an artifact to a specific excavation or acquisition history. Institutional authority reflects whether the organization’s core mission involves scholarly accuracy. Image-caption fidelity measures how reliably the text matches the visual. Licensing clarity determines whether you can legally use images in a K-8 curriculum. API availability determines whether you can automate verification at scale.

Sources that score highest on all six dimensions sit in Tier 1. A source that excels on some but fails on others drops to lower tiers. The critical insight for Project Euclid: **no single source covers all artifact types you need** (seals, tablets, ziggurats, reliefs, pottery, rosettes), so the hierarchy is designed for layered cross-referencing.

## Tier 3 — Curated secondary sources (verify against Tier 1 when possible)

### Smarthistory — BEST SECONDARY SOURCE

**URL:** https://smarthistory.org/ → Ancient Mediterranean → Ancient Near East

Content written by **PhD-holding art historians** (notably Dr. Senta German for ANE content) and produced in direct collaboration with the British Museum. Undergoes open peer review.  Covers Warka Vase, cylinder seals, Victory Stele of Naram-Sin, Ishtar Gate with video walkthroughs. **License: CC BY-NC-SA 4.0** — suitable for K-8 educational use.  **Caveat:** Individual embedded images carry their own licenses (some © Trustees of the British Museum). Smarthistory explicitly addresses Mesopotamian chronology complexity, which is good for RWI.

### Wikimedia Commons — USEFUL BUT REQUIRES PER-IMAGE VERIFICATION

Rich Mesopotamian categories: `Category:Art_of_Mesopotamia`, `Category:Cylinder_seals`, `Category:Ishtar_Gate`. Notable contributor **Osama Shukir Muhammed Amin FRCP(Glasg)** has uploaded hundreds of high-quality, properly attributed photographs under CC BY-SA 4.0. **However:** Anyone can upload and caption images. No editorial review for description accuracy. Verify every image by checking the “Source” field for a museum URL and cross-referencing the accession number against the museum’s own database.

### World History Encyclopedia

**URL:** https://www.worldhistory.org/

All content editorially reviewed before publication.  **License: CC BY-NC-SA.** Images are frequently sourced from Wikimedia Commons, meaning the same verification challenges apply. Good text descriptions but not always original photography. The **Burney Relief article** is an excellent case study — it properly discusses scholarly debate (Ishtar vs. Ereshkigal vs. Lilith) that lesser sources flatten.  

### University course pages and OER textbooks

Highly variable quality. Pressbooks and LibreTexts pages that reuse Smarthistory content under CC license are generally reliable. Student-created content on university wikis may contain errors. Course pages go offline frequently. Images may be used under Fair Use claims that don’t transfer to Project Euclid.

## Tier 4 — Use only with Tier 1 verification (or avoid entirely)

**These sources are dangerous for RWI integrity** because they systematically introduce text-image mismatches through algorithmic captioning, metadata-stripping, keyword optimization, and uncredited reconstructions.

**Google Images** generates captions algorithmically from surrounding page text, not from expert metadata. A Persepolis relief appearing on a page about “ancient Mesopotamian art” gets labeled Mesopotamian. Zero provenance guarantee. Mixes academic images, Pinterest repins, AI-generated content, and New Age conspiracy sites indiscriminately.

**Pinterest** strips metadata through chain-of-reposting. A board titled “Mesopotamian civilization” with 879 pins mixes Sumerian, Akkadian, Babylonian, Assyrian, and Persian artifacts without attribution. Descriptions like “Sumerian priests of E-Abzu, Eridu” are attached to modern reconstructions.   No editorial oversight whatsoever.

**Stock photo sites** (Shutterstock, Getty, Adobe Stock) optimize keywords for findability, not accuracy. Documented examples: Getty tags Persepolis Achaemenid reliefs as “ancient Mesopotamian art.”  Shutterstock returns cartoon ziggurat drawings, modern tattoo designs, and Persian ornaments alongside legitimate museum photographs for “Mesopotamia” queries. **26,000+ Shutterstock results for “Mesopotamia” — the vast majority are unusable for RWI.**

**Tourism sites** present modern reconstructions as ancient originals. Saddam Hussein’s 1980s reconstruction of Babylon appears in travel photos as “ancient Babylon.” The Berlin Ishtar Gate reconstruction is routinely described without noting that **less than 20% of its bricks are original** (per curator Helen Gries, Vorderasiatisches Museum).

## Automation guidance: APIs, IIIF, and verification pipelines

### Sources with public APIs (ranked by utility for Project Euclid)

|Source               |API Type   |Auth Required|License                 |Best For                                              |
|---------------------|-----------|-------------|------------------------|------------------------------------------------------|
|**Met Museum**       |REST       |None         |CC0                     |Primary image sourcing; ANE dept = departmentId 3     |
|**CDLI**             |REST + LOD |None         |Open (image rights vary)|Cuneiform tablets, seals; mathematical genre filtering|
|**Europeana**        |REST + IIIF|Free API key |Per-object              |European museum content (Louvre, Berlin)              |
|**DPLA**             |REST       |Free API key |Per-object              |US institutional collections                          |
|**Wikidata**         |SPARQL     |None         |CC0                     |Cross-referencing bridge between sources              |
|**Wikimedia Commons**|MediaWiki  |None         |Per-file                |Image metadata extraction and verification            |

### IIIF-supporting institutions

The Met, British Museum, Yale Peabody, Walters (manuscripts), Europeana, and Smithsonian all support IIIF. IIIF Image API enables **programmatic extraction of regions** — critical for cropping geometric details (e.g., isolating a rosette border from a large Assyrian relief panel).   Use Mirador viewer (https://projectmirador.org/) to load manifests from multiple institutions side-by-side for comparison.

### The cross-referencing pipeline: Wikidata as the bridge

The verification pipeline flows: **Wikimedia Commons Image → Wikidata Item → Museum Accession Number → Museum API → Verify metadata match.**

Wikidata properties essential for this pipeline: `P195` (collection/museum), `P217` (inventory/accession number), `P18` (canonical image), `P973` (described at URL → museum page), `P571` (inception date), `P186` (material). These properties link a Commons image back to its authoritative museum record.

**Example SPARQL query — all cylinder seals with images and museum records:**

```sparql
SELECT ?item ?itemLabel ?image ?collection ?collectionLabel ?inventoryNumber WHERE {
  ?item wdt:P31 wd:Q927436 .
  ?item wdt:P18 ?image .
  OPTIONAL { ?item wdt:P195 ?collection . }
  OPTIONAL { ?item wdt:P217 ?inventoryNumber . }
  SERVICE wikibase:label { bd:serviceParam wikibase:language "en" . }
} LIMIT 100
```

### Accession number formats for automated matching

|Museum             |Format                    |Example     |
|-------------------|--------------------------|------------|
|British Museum     |BM + number or YYYY-MMDD-N|BM 89115    |
|Metropolitan Museum|YY.NNN.N                  |31.13.1     |
|Yale Babylonian    |YBC + number              |YBC 7289    |
|Columbia (Plimpton)|Plimpton + number         |Plimpton 322|
|Berlin VAM         |VA or VAT + number        |VAT 6598    |
|Louvre             |AO + number               |AO 17264    |
|CDLI               |P + 6 digits              |P254790     |

### Escalation protocol for RWI verification

When an image is found at Tier 3 or below, the system should automatically escalate:

1. **Extract identifiers** from the image’s metadata or caption — artifact name, museum name, accession number, period, culture
1. **Search CDLI** (for cuneiform objects) using the accession number or artifact description via `cdli.earth/docs/api`
1. **Search Wikidata** via SPARQL using `P217` (inventory number) to find the canonical Wikidata item
1. **Query museum API** (Met first, then BM collection search) using the accession number to pull the authoritative record
1. **Compare fields:** title, date/period, culture, medium, dimensions between the Tier 3 source and Tier 1 record
1. **Flag discrepancies** for human review — any mismatch in period, culture, deity attribution, or “reconstruction” status triggers a hold

## Search strategies for Project Euclid’s geometric artifact categories

### Cylinder seals and impressions

**Primary sources:** British Museum (world’s largest collection), Met API (`departmentId=3&q=cylinder+seal`), CDLI (search artifact type = “seal”), Yale Babylonian (4,000 seals + 10,000 impressions), Morgan Library. **Search terms:** “cylinder seal” + “geometric,” “guilloche,” “rosette,” “interlocking circles,” “chevron.” Focus on seals showing rotational symmetry, nested shapes, and repeating border patterns for geometric curriculum use.

### Mathematical cuneiform tablets

**Plimpton 322** is at **Columbia University** (Rare Book & Manuscript Library),  not Yale — authoritative images at ISAW NYU exhibit: https://isaw.nyu.edu/exhibitions/before-pythagoras/items/plimpton-322/. **YBC 7289** (√2 tablet) is at Yale — 3D scan on Sketchfab. Use CDLI with genre filter “mathematical” to find all catalogued mathematical tablets.  ORACC sub-project DCCMT (http://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/dccmt/) provides annotated editions.

### Ziggurat reconstructions and plans

**Critical distinction for RWI:** Archaeological plans have scale bars, section markers, and grid references. Artistic reconstructions are attributed to specific illustrators (Balage Balogh, Jean-Claude Golvin). CGI reconstructions (e.g., Artefacts/DAI Uruk ziggurat) circulate without their digital-reconstruction status noted. **Best sources:** ISAC excavation archives for archaeological plans; Smarthistory for properly credited reconstructions. **Always label:** “reconstruction,” “archaeological plan,” or “photograph of extant remains.”

### Ishtar Gate reliefs and rosettes

**Primary collection:** Vorderasiatisches Museum Berlin (Pergamon, currently closed).  **Online access:** SMB collections at https://recherche.smb.museum/ (search “Ischtar-Tor”) or via Europeana API. The Met owns Processional Way striding lion panels (accession 31.13.1, 31.13.2). Wikimedia Commons `Category:Ishtar_Gate` has extensive CC-licensed photographs. **For rosette motifs:** search “rosette” + “Babylon” + “Nebuchadnezzar” across all platforms.

### Sumerian geometric pottery patterns

**Best periods:** Ubaid (5500–4000 BCE), Halaf (6100–5100 BCE), Samarra (5500–4800 BCE) — all feature distinctive geometric painted pottery. **Search terms:** “Ubaid pottery geometric,” “Halaf pottery pattern,” “Samarra ware.” **Best sources:** British Museum, Penn Museum (Ur excavation material), Louvre (Tello/Girsu, Susa pottery), ISAC (Nippur, Khafajah material).

### Assyrian palace reliefs with geometric borders

**Primary collection:** British Museum — the world’s largest collection from Nimrud, Nineveh, and Khorsabad. **Search for geometric elements:** “guilloche border” + “Assyrian,” “palmette” + “Nimrud,” “sacred tree” + “Ashurnasirpal.” Search by palace: Northwest Palace (Ashurnasirpal II), Southwest Palace (Sennacherib), North Palace (Ashurbanipal). Met API: `departmentId=3&q=assyrian+relief&hasImages=true`.

### Star and rosette motifs (Ishtar’s 8-pointed star)

The **8-pointed star of Ishtar/Inanna** demonstrates rotational symmetry, compass construction, and octagonal geometry — directly relevant to Sami’s Ishtar=8-Star pairing. **Best sources:** Kudurru stones (Kassite boundary markers) at the British Museum feature deity symbols including star motifs. Ishtar Gate rosette borders (Berlin/Europeana). **Search terms:** “eight pointed star Mesopotamia,” “star of Ishtar,” “kudurru star symbol.” Wikimedia: `Category:Star_of_Ishtar`, `Category:Kudurru`.

## Known pitfalls: where text-image fidelity breaks down

### Period misattribution is pervasive

“Sumerian” is used as a catch-all for everything Mesopotamian. Neo-Assyrian reliefs (9th–7th c. BCE) get labeled “Babylonian.” Akkadian-period objects (c. 2334–2154 BCE) are mislabeled as Sumerian. The Burney Relief / Queen of the Night is sometimes called “Sumerian” despite being Old Babylonian (c. 1800–1750 BCE).  **For Project Euclid’s deity pairings:** Ensure period-correct attribution — Shamash, Ishtar, and Enlil span multiple periods, but their iconographic representations changed significantly across Sumerian, Akkadian, and Neo-Babylonian eras.

### The reconstruction trap is everywhere

**The Ishtar Gate in Berlin is less than 20% original Babylonian bricks** — the rest is modern reconstruction (per curator Helen Gries). The Processional Way is ~45% original; the Throne Room Façade only ~15%.  Saddam Hussein’s 1980s Babylon reconstruction added modern bricks stamped with his name atop ancient foundations — tourist photos show this modern layer, not antiquity. CGI ziggurat reconstructions circulate without their digital origin noted. **RWI rule: Every reconstruction image must be explicitly labeled as such.**

### Cylinder seal mirror-image confusion

Seals are carved in intaglio (negative) so impressions appear in positive relief. **The intended image is the impression, which is a mirror of the seal surface.** Images showing the seal itself display “backwards” cuneiform — this is correct but counter-intuitive. Well-meaning editors sometimes flip images, creating errors. Museums typically show both seal and impression side by side, but when only one circulates online, orientation confusion is inevitable. **For Project Euclid: Always specify whether showing “seal” or “impression” and note the reversal.**

### Deity misidentification and the Burney Relief problem

The Burney Relief is identified as Ishtar, Ereshkigal, OR Lilith depending on the source. The British Museum prefers Ereshkigal; Thorkild Jacobsen argued Ishtar; the Lilith identification is largely discredited but enormously popular online. On the Ishtar Gate: lions = Ishtar, **mušḫuššu dragons = Marduk** (not “Ishtar’s dragon”), aurochs = Adad.  Lamassu (winged bulls) are characteristically Assyrian, not Babylonian, despite frequent mislabeling.

### Persian sites masquerade as Mesopotamian

**This is one of the most common and pernicious errors.** Getty Images tags Persepolis reliefs as “ancient Mesopotamian art.” Achaemenid art deliberately adopted Mesopotamian motifs (lamassu-style figures, glazed brick techniques), making visual confusion understandable but factually wrong. **RWI rule: Anything from Persepolis (Iran), Susa (Iran), or Hattusa (Turkey) must be excluded from Mesopotamian content**, regardless of stylistic similarity.

### AI-generated artifacts are an accelerating threat

The Ure Museum of Greek Archaeology mounted an exhibition titled “Distorted History: AI’s Skewed Visions of the Ancient World” documenting the problem. AI-generated Mesopotamian images characteristically merge motifs from different periods, create impossibly well-preserved surfaces, invent plausible-looking but gibberish cuneiform, and blend Assyrian, Babylonian, and Persian elements indiscriminately. **RWI rule: Any image not traceable to a specific museum, excavation, or professional photographer should be treated as potentially AI-generated.**

## Conclusion: the practical workflow for Sami

The hierarchy works as a **funnel**. Start every image search at the Met API (CC0, automated, department 3) and CDLI (cuneiform-specific, genre-filterable). When those don’t have what you need, check the British Museum collection online and Europeana (which aggregates Berlin and Louvre). For secondary context, use Smarthistory first — its ANE content is British Museum-partnered and PhD-authored. If a Tier 3 image is the only option, run the Wikidata escalation protocol to verify the accession number against a Tier 1 record before allowing it into the curriculum.

Three non-obvious insights emerged from this research. First, **Plimpton 322 is at Columbia, not Yale** — this misattribution is so widespread it will likely appear in Sami’s own source materials. Second, the Pergamon Museum’s complete closure until ~2037 means Berlin’s Ishtar Gate holdings are accessible only digitally via Europeana and SMB’s online portal — making programmatic access through Europeana’s API strategically important. Third, Wikidata’s structured properties (especially P217 for accession numbers and P973 for museum URLs) make it the most efficient cross-referencing bridge for automated verification — building Project Euclid’s RWI pipeline around Wikidata SPARQL queries will scale better than institution-by-institution verification.
